Electric vehicle charging infrastructure refers to the network of equipment that supplies electrical energy to recharge plug-in electric vehicles, including battery electric vehicles and plug-in hybrids. Charging is generally categorized into three levels: Level 1 uses a standard 120-volt household outlet and can take over 24 hours for a full charge; Level 2 uses a 240-volt circuit similar to a household dryer outlet and typically charges a vehicle in 4 to 8 hours; and DC fast charging can restore 80% of battery capacity in 20 to 40 minutes by supplying direct current at much higher power levels.

A major barrier to EV adoption in many regions, including Ontario, is uneven distribution of charging infrastructure. Urban centers tend to have far denser charging networks than rural and suburban areas, creating so-called charging deserts where residents without home charging access face significant inconvenience. Multi-unit residential buildings pose a particular challenge, since many older apartment and condo buildings were not wired to support widespread Level 2 charging, requiring costly electrical upgrades to shared infrastructure.

Charging networks are operated by a mix of automakers, utilities, and third-party charging companies, leading to fragmentation in payment systems, connector standards, and reliability reporting. In North America, the industry has been consolidating around the North American Charging Standard (NACS) connector, originally developed by Tesla, after most major automakers announced plans to adopt it, which is expected to reduce compatibility issues between vehicles and charging networks over the next several years.

Government incentive programs, such as rebates for home charger installation and grants for public charging deployment, have been used to accelerate buildout, but critics argue that grid capacity upgrades have not kept pace with charger installation targets in some jurisdictions, risking bottlenecks as EV adoption increases.
